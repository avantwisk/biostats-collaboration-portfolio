SECTION 1: CONCEPTUAL QUESTIONS ABOUT ADEMP
================================================================================

Q1: Why do we need a framework like ADEMP? Can't we just write simulation code?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ANSWER: Absolutely you can write code without ADEMP, but research shows that
unstructured simulations often suffer from methodological flaws—selective
scenario reporting, unclear estimands, unfair method comparisons, and missing
uncertainty quantification. ADEMP provides a principled workflow that prevents
these pitfalls by forcing you to think through each component systematically.
It's like having a pre-specified protocol for your simulation study, similar
to how we register clinical trials. The framework ensures transparency,
reproducibility, and defensibility when reviewers scrutinize your work.

REFERENCE: Slide 4 (ADEMP Overview), Slide 18 (Pitfalls)


Q2: Do the ADEMP components have to be completed in order?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ANSWER: Conceptually, yes—ADEMP is designed as a logical sequence. Your Aims
drive what Data you need to generate and what Estimands matter. The Estimand
determines which Methods are appropriate for comparison. Performance measures
must align with your original Aims. However, in practice there's iteration:
pilot simulations might reveal that your DGM needs refinement, or that certain
methods fail too often. The key is that your *final* design should reflect
coherent flow from A→D→E→M→P, even if you circled back during development.

REFERENCE: Slide 4 (ADEMP Overview)


Q3: How is the Estimand different from what we're estimating (the estimate)?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ANSWER: The *estimand* is the TRUE target parameter you want to learn about—
it's defined by your data-generating mechanism. Examples include a population
risk difference, a hazard ratio, a median survival time. The *estimator* is
the METHOD you use to estimate it (e.g., a regression coefficient, a Kaplan-
Meier median, a propensity-score-adjusted difference). The *estimate* is the
numerical result you get from applying that estimator to one dataset. In
simulations, we know the true estimand by design, which lets us directly
measure how well each estimator performs (bias, MSE, coverage). Confusing
these leads to unfair method comparisons—like comparing a marginal effect
to a conditional effect when they're answering different questions.

REFERENCE: Slide 7 (Estimands), Slide 10-13 (Worked Example)


Q4: What if my research question doesn't fit neatly into ADEMP?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ANSWER: ADEMP is flexible enough to accommodate most simulation purposes. If
you're doing exploratory work—say, investigating a new phenomenon without
pre-specified hypotheses—you can frame your Aim as "explore the behavior of
Method X under conditions Y." If you're comparing algorithms rather than
statistical estimators, your Estimand might be "classification accuracy" or
"convergence rate." The framework adapts; the key is being explicit about
what you're testing, how you generate data, what you're targeting, and how
you measure success. If you find ADEMP truly doesn't fit, ask whether your
study is actually a *simulation study* or something else (like a software
benchmark or pure algorithm development).

REFERENCE: Slide 5-9 (ADEMP Components)


================================================================================
SECTION 2: DATA-GENERATING MECHANISMS (DGM)
================================================================================

Q5: How do I choose realistic parameters for my data-generating mechanism?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ANSWER: Start with pilot data, published literature, or domain experts. For
example, if simulating a clinical trial, look at historical trials in similar
populations to inform baseline event rates, dropout patterns, and effect sizes.
If you're testing a new method, calibrate your DGM to published data summaries.
The goal isn't to match one specific dataset perfectly, but to span a plausible
range. It's also wise to include some extreme scenarios as stress tests—low
sample sizes, high missingness, strong departures from assumptions—to see
where methods break down. Document your parameter choices and their sources
transparently.

REFERENCE: Slide 6 (Data-generating Mechanisms), Slide 14 (Sensitivity &
Robustness)


Q6: Should I use parametric data generation or resample from real data?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ANSWER: It depends on your aims. Parametric DGMs give you control and
flexibility—you can systematically vary sample sizes, effect sizes, and
distributions to stress-test methods across many scenarios. Resampling from
real data keeps you grounded in reality but limits generalizability to that
one observed dataset. In practice, many simulation studies use parametric
DGMs informed by pilot data or literature estimates to balance realism and
breadth. For sensitivity analyses, you might do both: parametric as your
primary approach, then resample from real data as a robustness check.

REFERENCE: Slide 6 (DGM), Slide 10 (Worked Example - realistic parameters)


Q7: How many scenarios should I include in my factorial design?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ANSWER: Enough to capture the key factors affecting method performance, but
not so many that results become uninterpretable. Start with 2-4 factors at
2-3 levels each. For example: sample size (n=50, 100, 200), dropout (0%, 10%,
20%), effect size (small, medium, large). That's 3×3×3 = 27 scenarios. Pre-
specify which scenarios are primary (testing main hypotheses) vs exploratory
(sensitivity analyses). If the full factorial is too large, use fractional
factorial or Latin hypercube designs. The key is avoiding selective reporting—
don't cherry-pick scenarios where your method looks good. Report all
pre-specified scenarios or justify exclusions transparently.

REFERENCE: Slide 6 (DGM), Slide 14 (Sensitivity), Slide 18 (Pitfalls)


Q8: Can I add complexity to my DGM during the study, or is that "cheating"?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ANSWER: Adding scenarios mid-study isn't inherently wrong, but it must be done
transparently. If pilot results reveal an important case you hadn't considered,
you can add it—but label it clearly as post-hoc or exploratory in your report.
The danger is "scenario shopping": running many configurations and reporting
only those where your method wins. The antidote is pre-registration (if
feasible) or transparent reporting of what was planned vs added. Think of it
like pre-specified vs exploratory analyses in clinical trials—both are valid,
but readers need to know which is which.

REFERENCE: Slide 18 (Pitfalls - selective scenarios)

SECTION 3: METHODS & FAIRNESS
================================================================================

Q9: How do I ensure a fair comparison between methods?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ANSWER: Fairness requires symmetry across five dimensions:
1. Same estimand (don't compare conditional vs marginal effects)
2. Same data generation (all methods see identical simulated datasets)
3. Equal tuning effort (if you optimize hyperparameters for Method A, do the
   same for Method B)
4. Same convergence/failure rules (if Method A gets 3 retries, so does B)
5. Same evaluation metrics (use the same performance measures for all)

Document these choices explicitly. Report convergence failures for all methods,
not just successes. If one method requires more computational resources, report
that as part of the trade-off analysis. The goal isn't to make methods look
identical, but to give each a fair shot under neutral conditions.

REFERENCE: Slide 8 (Methods), Slide 16 (Neutral Method Comparison)


Q10: What do I do when a method fails to converge in some simulations?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ANSWER: First, define failure rules upfront: e.g., "allow one retry with
different starting values; if still fails, record as non-convergence." Apply
these rules symmetrically to all methods. Then report failure rates as a
performance metric—high failure rates are informative! They tell you a method
is fragile in certain scenarios. For primary metrics like bias and coverage,
you can either (a) exclude failures and report results conditional on
convergence, or (b) use multiple imputation or worst-case/best-case bounds.
The key is transparency: show readers how many iterations failed and whether
results are sensitive to those exclusions.

REFERENCE: Slide 8 (Methods - Fair Comparison), Slide 18 (Pitfalls - hidden
failures)


Q11: Can I use different software implementations for different methods?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ANSWER: Yes, but acknowledge potential implementation differences. For example,
if comparing R's glm() to Python's statsmodels, small numerical differences
might arise from optimization algorithms or convergence criteria. Document
which packages and versions you used. If possible, verify that both
implementations give equivalent results on a test case where the answer is
known. If methods are only available in different languages, that's fine—just
be transparent about it. Implementation differences are real-world constraints;
hiding them would be worse than acknowledging them.

REFERENCE: Slide 8 (Methods), Slide 19 (Reporting - session info)

SECTION 4: PERFORMANCE MEASURES & MONTE CARLO SE
================================================================================

Q12: What is Monte Carlo Standard Error (MCSE) and why does it matter?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ANSWER: MCSE quantifies the sampling variability in your simulation results.
Even though you control the data-generating process, your performance estimates
(bias, power, coverage) are themselves random because they're based on a finite
number of simulation repetitions (nsim). MCSE tells you how much those
estimates would bounce around if you re-ran the simulation with a different
random seed. For example, if you estimate power = 0.82 with MCSE = 0.01, the
true power (for infinite repetitions) is roughly 0.82 ± 0.02. Always report
MCSEs—they let readers judge whether differences between methods are real or
just simulation noise. Omitting MCSEs is like reporting a point estimate
without a confidence interval in a real study.

REFERENCE: Slide 17 (Performance - MCSE), Slide 13 (Worked Example - power
curve with MCSE)


Q13: How do I calculate Monte Carlo SE for different performance measures?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ANSWER: It depends on the metric:

- Power (proportion): MCSE ≈ sqrt(p*(1-p) / nsim), where p is estimated power
- Coverage (proportion): same formula as power
- Bias (mean difference): MCSE = SD(estimates) / sqrt(nsim), where SD is the
  sample standard deviation of your point estimates across repetitions
- MSE: Use bootstrap or delta method; see Morris et al. 2019 for formulas

For most proportions, if you want MCSE ≤ 0.01, you need nsim ≥ 2,000 (because
at worst, when p=0.5, MCSE = sqrt(0.25/nsim) ≈ 0.01 requires nsim ≥ 2,500).

REFERENCE: Slide 17 (MCSE Visual), Slide 12 (Worked Example - choosing nsim)


Q14: How many simulation repetitions (nsim) should I run?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ANSWER: Choose nsim to make your MCSE small enough for decision-making. Rules
of thumb:
- For power/coverage: nsim ≥ 2,000 gives MCSE ≈ 0.01 (acceptable for most uses)
- For rare events (Type I error at α=0.05): nsim ≥ 10,000 gives MCSE ≈ 0.002
- For precise bias estimation: depends on variability, but nsim ≥ 5,000 is common

Don't just pick nsim=1,000 because it's a round number. Justify your choice
based on target MCSE and the performance metric that matters most for your aims.
Remember: doubling nsim only reduces MCSE by 1/sqrt(2) ≈ 0.71, so there are
diminishing returns. Balance precision against computational cost.

REFERENCE: Slide 12 (Worked Example - loop logic), Slide 17 (MCSE shrinks
with more repetitions)


Q15: What performance measures should I report?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ANSWER: It depends on your aims, but core metrics include:
- Bias: accuracy (systematic error)
- MSE or RMSE: total deviation combining bias and variance
- Coverage: do confidence intervals contain the true value at the nominal rate?
- Power: probability of correctly rejecting a false null hypothesis
- Type I error: false positive rate under the null (should match nominal α)

Secondary metrics might include: computational time, convergence rate,
robustness to violations. Always include MCSEs for the primary metrics. Use
visualizations (power curves, bias heatmaps, coverage plots) rather than
massive tables—patterns are easier to see in graphs.

REFERENCE: Slide 9 (Performance Overview), Slide 13-17 (Performance Measures
Detailed)

SECTION 5: PRACTICAL IMPLEMENTATION
================================================================================

Q16: How long does a simulation study take to run?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ANSWER: It varies enormously. A simple two-sample t-test comparison with
nsim=2,000 across 10 scenarios might run in minutes. A bootstrap-heavy method
or complex mixed model across 50 scenarios with nsim=5,000 could take hours or
days. Strategies to manage runtime:

1. Parallelize across scenarios or repetitions (use R packages like furrr,
   future, or parallel)
2. Profile your code to find bottlenecks (use profvis in R)
3. Start with a small pilot (nsim=100) to debug, then scale up
4. Use high-performance computing (HPC cluster) for large studies
5. Cache intermediate results so you don't lose progress if jobs crash

Report runtime as part of your results—it's a legitimate performance metric
when comparing methods in practice.

REFERENCE: Slide 12 (Worked Example - loop logic), Slide 9 (Performance -
runtime as metric)


Q17: What software should I use for simulation studies?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ANSWER: The language matters less than reproducibility and clarity. Popular
choices:
- R: excellent ecosystem (tidyverse, furrr, broom, ggplot2), great for stats
- Python: good for ML methods, integration with other tools
- Julia: fast for computationally intensive simulations
- Stata: if your audience uses Stata, simulating in Stata aids adoption

For reporting, Quarto (or R Markdown) is ideal—it integrates code, results,
and narrative, making your study fully reproducible. Key practices:
- Set seeds explicitly and document them
- Use version control (Git)
- Share code and data (or synthetic data if privacy is a concern)
- Record session info (package versions, R version, OS)

REFERENCE: Slide 19 (Reporting & visuals), Slide 20 (Checklist - code/seeds)


Q18: Should I use parallel computing for my simulations?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ANSWER: Yes, if runtime is an issue. Simulation studies are "embarrassingly
parallel"—each repetition is independent, so you can split them across CPU
cores or cluster nodes with near-linear speedup. In R, use:
- furrr + future for easy parallelization
- parallel package for lower-level control
- Batch jobs on HPC clusters for very large studies

Set separate seeds for each parallel worker to ensure reproducibility. Be
aware of memory constraints—each worker needs its own copy of data and
functions. Always test your parallel code on a small scale before launching
massive jobs.

REFERENCE: Slide 12 (Worked Example - loop logic)


Q19: How do I organize my simulation code for reproducibility?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ANSWER: Use a project structure like:
/
├── R/
│   ├── simulation_helpers.R    # DGM functions
│   ├── visualization_helpers.R # Plotting functions
├── config/
│   └── scenarios.csv            # Parameter grid
├── output/
│   ├── results_raw.rds          # Simulation output
│   └── results_summary.csv      # Aggregated metrics
├── figures/
├── presentation.qmd             # Quarto doc with analysis
├── run_simulation.R             # Main script
└── README.md                    # Instructions

Key principles:
- Separate data generation, analysis, and visualization into functions
- Use config files for scenarios (easier to modify than hardcoded values)
- Save intermediate results (don't re-run everything to tweak a plot)
- Document seeds, package versions, runtime
- Version control with Git, share on GitHub/OSF

REFERENCE: Slide 19 (Reporting), Slide 20 (Checklist)

SECTION 6: COMPARISON WITH OTHER APPROACHES
================================================================================

Q20: When should I use simulation vs. closed-form (formula-based) calculations?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ANSWER: Use formulas when:
- Closed-form solutions exist and assumptions are clearly met
- You need a quick, back-of-the-envelope answer
- The setting is simple (e.g., standard two-sample t-test, balanced design)

Use simulation when:
- Assumptions are questionable (small samples, non-normality, complex missing
  data)
- No formula exists (complex designs, novel methods)
- You want to test robustness across many scenarios
- You need to incorporate realistic nuisance factors (dropout, clustering,
  measurement error)

In practice, do BOTH: use formulas as a starting point, then simulate to
verify and extend. The worked example in the presentation shows this: the
formula gave N=182 per arm, but simulation accounting for dropout and using
the actual test statistic suggested N=196—a meaningful difference.

REFERENCE: Slide 10 (Worked Example - formula method), Slide 13 (Power curve
comparison)


Q21: How does ADEMP compare to other simulation frameworks?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ANSWER: ADEMP (Morris et al. 2019) is one of several frameworks, but it's
particularly well-suited for biostatistics because it emphasizes estimands
explicitly—a concept central to modern causal inference and trial design. Other
frameworks include:
- Burton et al. (2006): Earlier guidance on simulation study design in medical
  statistics
- STRESS guidelines: Focused on reporting standards for simulation studies
- Boulesteix et al. (2020): Introduction to simulation in health research

ADEMP's strength is its clear workflow and connection to the ICH E9(R1)
estimand framework. It's not radically different from these alternatives—think
of it as a synthesis and refinement of best practices with a memorable acronym.

REFERENCE: Slide 21 (References), Slide 4 (ADEMP Overview)


Q22: Can I use ADEMP for machine learning method comparisons?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ANSWER: Absolutely. The framework adapts naturally:
- Aims: "Compare predictive accuracy of random forests vs gradient boosting"
- Data: Generate from a known regression/classification function with realistic
  noise and covariate distributions
- Estimands: "Prediction error" or "AUC" on test data
- Methods: RF and GBM with equal tuning effort (e.g., same CV procedure)
- Performance: MSE, AUC, calibration, plus MCSE from repeated train/test splits

The key is fairness: give both methods the same amount of hyperparameter tuning,
the same preprocessing, and the same training/test split protocol. Report
computational time as well, since ML methods often trade accuracy for speed.

REFERENCE: Slide 8 (Methods - Fair Comparison), Slide 16 (Method Comparison)

SECTION 7: REPORTING & PUBLICATION
================================================================================

Q23: What should I include in the Methods section of my paper?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ANSWER: At minimum:
1. ADEMP table (one row per component, summarizing your design)
2. Description of data-generating mechanisms with parameter values and sources
3. Estimands and their interpretation
4. Methods compared, including software/packages/versions
5. Failure handling rules
6. Performance metrics and how MCSEs were calculated
7. Number of simulation repetitions (nsim) and justification
8. Random seeds or seed-setting procedure
9. Statement about code/data availability

The ADEMP table is a concise, scannable summary—reviewers love it. Put detailed
formulas and code in appendices or supplementary materials. Link to a public
repository (GitHub, OSF) for full reproducibility.

REFERENCE: Slide 19 (Reporting & visuals), Slide 20 (Checklist)


Q24: How should I visualize simulation results?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ANSWER: Prefer plots over tables for pattern recognition:
- Power curves with MCSE ribbons (see slide 13)
- Bias heatmaps showing scenarios × methods (see slide 6 factorial design)
- Coverage bar charts with target reference lines (see slide 16)
- Small multiples for sensitivity analyses (different parameter values side-
  by-side)

For tables, keep them compact:
- Use rounding appropriate to MCSE (don't report bias to 6 decimals if MCSE
  is 0.01)
- Highlight cells that violate performance thresholds (e.g., coverage < 93%)
- Include MCSEs in parentheses next to estimates

Always include figure captions explaining what "good" looks like (e.g., "bars
should reach dashed line for nominal 95% coverage").

REFERENCE: Slide 19 (Reporting & visuals), Slide 13-17 (Performance visuals)


Q25: Do I need to pre-register my simulation study?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ANSWER: Pre-registration isn't required for simulation studies the way it is
for clinical trials, but it's increasingly encouraged—especially for
methodological comparisons where there's risk of bias favoring your own method.
Platforms like OSF allow you to time-stamp your protocol before running
simulations. Benefits:
- Prevents selective scenario reporting and p-hacking
- Increases trust in your findings
- Distinguishes confirmatory from exploratory results

If full pre-registration feels too rigid, at least document your original plan
and transparently report any deviations (e.g., "we added a sensitivity analysis
after reviewer request"). The goal is transparency, not perfection.

REFERENCE: Slide 18 (Pitfalls - pre-specify, register)


Q26: What if reviewers ask for additional scenarios after submission?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ANSWER: This is common and usually reasonable. Reviewers may spot scenarios
you didn't consider. If the request is fair (e.g., testing robustness to a
plausible assumption), add it and label it as post-hoc. If it's unreasonable
(e.g., "test 100 new scenarios"), push back politely: explain that your
original design was pre-specified to balance comprehensiveness and feasibility,
and offer a compromise (e.g., add 2-3 targeted scenarios in revision).

Because you used ADEMP and documented everything clearly, adding scenarios is
straightforward—your code structure makes it easy to plug in new parameters
and re-run. This is a huge advantage of the framework's modularity.

REFERENCE: Slide 20 (Checklist - flexible code structure)

SECTION 8: PITFALLS & QUESTIONABLE RESEARCH PRACTICES
================================================================================

Q27: What is "seed shopping" and why is it bad?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ANSWER: Seed shopping is trying multiple random seeds and reporting only the
one that makes your method look best. It's analogous to p-hacking in empirical
research—running many analyses and reporting the most favorable. This is
scientific misconduct. The antidote:
- Set your seed(s) upfront and document them
- If you accidentally discover a "bad seed" (e.g., one that causes unusual
  failures), investigate why (it might reveal a real problem!), but don't
  silently switch to another seed
- Use multiple independent runs with different seeds as a robustness check,
  and report all of them—variability across seeds is informative

Random number generators are deterministic given a seed, so fixing seeds makes
your results exactly reproducible. Transparency about seeds is a hallmark of
credible simulation research.

REFERENCE: Slide 18 (Pitfalls - seed shopping)


Q28: How do I avoid unfair tuning when comparing methods?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ANSWER: Give every method the same tuning budget:
- If you do 5-fold CV for Method A, do it for Method B too
- If you try 10 hyperparameter combinations for Method A, do the same for B
- Use the same training/validation split
- Document the tuning procedure in your methods section

If one method truly requires more tuning to work well (e.g., neural networks
often need extensive architecture search), acknowledge this as a practical
limitation and report it transparently. The goal isn't to handicap complex
methods, but to prevent giving your favorite method an unfair advantage through
excessive hand-tuning while leaving competitors at default settings.

REFERENCE: Slide 8 (Methods - Fair Comparison), Slide 18 (Pitfalls)


Q29: What if my new method performs worse than existing methods in simulation?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ANSWER: Report it honestly! Negative results are valuable for the field—they
prevent others from wasting time on the same approach. Frame it constructively:
- Explain why you expected the method to work
- Identify which scenarios it struggles with and why
- Discuss potential modifications that might improve performance
- Emphasize what was learned (even "failed" methods teach us something)

Simulation studies should be about finding truth, not salesmanship for your
method. Reviewers and readers respect honesty. If your method only works in
a narrow set of scenarios, say so—that's useful guidance for practitioners.

REFERENCE: Slide 18 (Pitfalls - selective reporting)


Q30: How can I make my simulation study reproducible?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ANSWER: Follow this checklist:
1. Share code (GitHub, OSF, journal supplements)
2. Document all random seeds
3. Record software versions (sessionInfo() in R, pip freeze in Python)
4. Use relative paths and package management (renv in R, virtual environments
   in Python)
5. Include a README explaining how to reproduce results
6. Archive dependencies (in case packages change)
7. Provide either raw data (if simulated) or code to regenerate it

Quarto/R Markdown documents are ideal because they interleave code, results,
and narrative. Someone should be able to clone your repository, run one script,
and obtain identical results (up to numerical precision). Reproducibility
builds trust and enables others to extend your work.

REFERENCE: Slide 19 (Reporting), Slide 20 (Checklist - code, seeds, session
info)

SECTION 9: ADVANCED TOPICS & EXTENSIONS
================================================================================

Q31: Can ADEMP handle complex study designs like cluster-randomized trials?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ANSWER: Yes. The framework scales to complex designs:
- Aims: "Estimate required number of clusters for 80% power"
- Data: Generate clusters with intra-cluster correlation (ICC), then randomize
  at cluster level; simulate individual outcomes within clusters
- Estimands: Cluster-level treatment effect or population-average effect
- Methods: GEE, mixed models, cluster-robust inference
- Performance: Power, bias, coverage (accounting for clustering in SEs)

The key is being explicit about your DGM parameters (ICC, cluster size
variability, between-cluster variance) and ensuring methods target the same
estimand (marginal vs cluster-specific effects). Simulation is especially
valuable here because closed-form power calculations often make simplifying
assumptions (equal cluster sizes, no missing data) that rarely hold in practice.

REFERENCE: Slide 6 (DGM - realistic complexity), Slide 7 (Estimands -
marginal vs conditional)


Q32: How do I simulate missing data realistically?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ANSWER: Start by defining the missing data mechanism:
- MCAR (Missing Completely At Random): missingness independent of observed or
  unobserved data
- MAR (Missing At Random): missingness depends on observed data only
- MNAR (Missing Not At Random): missingness depends on unobserved values

For MAR, build a model for missingness probability as a function of observed
covariates (e.g., logistic regression: P(missing) ~ age + treatment). For MNAR,
make missingness depend on the outcome itself (e.g., sicker patients more
likely to drop out). Then:
1. Generate complete data
2. Apply missingness mechanism
3. Analyze with complete-case, multiple imputation, or likelihood-based methods
4. Compare performance

This lets you test whether methods are robust to realistic missingness patterns
and whether missing data assumptions (MAR vs MNAR) affect conclusions.

REFERENCE: Slide 6 (DGM - realistic nuisance factors), Slide 10 (Worked
Example - dropout)


Q33: Can I use simulation to validate a new statistical test?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ANSWER: Absolutely—that's a classic use case. Your ADEMP plan might be:
- Aims: "Verify that the new test achieves nominal Type I error (α=0.05) and
  has adequate power"
- Data: Generate under the null (to check α) and under alternatives (to check
  power)
- Estimands: True null hypothesis or specific effect size
- Methods: New test vs established alternatives (e.g., t-test, permutation test)
- Performance: Empirical Type I error (should ≈ 0.05), power, runtime

Run this across a grid of sample sizes and effect sizes. If your new test
shows inflated Type I error (e.g., 0.08 instead of 0.05), that's a red flag—
it's not controlling the error rate. Report these findings transparently.

REFERENCE: Slide 5 (Aims - define primary question), Slide 9 (Performance -
Type I error)


Q34: How do I handle convergence issues in complex models like mixed effects?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ANSWER: Pre-specify your protocol:
1. Start with reasonable initial values
2. Allow one retry with different starting values or optimizer
3. Record as "non-convergence" if still fails
4. Report convergence rates for all methods

For mixed models, use:
- Multiple optimizers (e.g., bobyqa, Nelder-Mead in R's lme4)
- Simplify random effects structure if full model won't converge
- Check for singular fits (estimated variance = 0)

High non-convergence rates are informative—they tell you the method struggles
with small samples or certain data structures. Don't hide these failures; they're
part of the performance profile. You might report metrics both including and
excluding non-converged iterations to show sensitivity.

REFERENCE: Slide 8 (Methods - convergence rules), Slide 18 (Pitfalls - hidden
failures)


Q35: Can I use bootstrap or permutation methods inside my simulation?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ANSWER: Yes, but be aware of computational cost. If your method involves
bootstrapping (e.g., bootstrap confidence intervals), each simulation repetition
requires hundreds of bootstrap resamples—this nested resampling can be slow.
Strategies:
- Parallelize both outer (simulation) and inner (bootstrap) loops
- Use fewer bootstrap resamples (B=200-500 may suffice for CIs)
- Consider using profiling to optimize bottlenecks
- Use HPC resources if available

For permutation tests, similar logic applies: each sim rep requires many
permutations. Budget your computational time accordingly. It's valid to compare
a bootstrap method to a parametric method as long as both see the same
simulated datasets and you apply them consistently.

REFERENCE: Slide 12 (Loop Logic), Slide 16 (Computational considerations)

SECTION 10: CONTEXTUAL & PHILOSOPHICAL QUESTIONS
================================================================================

Q36: Isn't simulation just "making up data"? How is that scientific?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ANSWER: Simulation is "making up data" in a very specific, controlled way where
we know the truth by design. This is precisely what makes it scientific: we can
test methods under known conditions, measure how far estimates deviate from
truth, and build understanding of when methods work or fail. It's analogous to
laboratory experiments in chemistry—you control conditions to isolate effects.
The key is transparency: document your DGM, justify parameter choices with
literature or pilot data, and don't cherry-pick scenarios. Done right,
simulation provides rigorous evidence about method performance that complements
(but doesn't replace) theoretical analysis and real-data validation.

REFERENCE: Slide 3 (Why Simulate?)


Q37: Should I always trust simulation results over mathematical theory?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ANSWER: No—they're complementary. Mathematical theory provides guarantees
(e.g., "this estimator is unbiased asymptotically"), but often under idealized
conditions. Simulation tests whether those conditions hold in practice with
finite samples, missing data, and violated assumptions. Ideally:
- Theory tells you what SHOULD happen
- Simulation tells you what DOES happen in realistic settings
- Real data analysis tells you what ACTUALLY happens

If simulation contradicts theory, investigate: maybe your DGM is misspecified,
your code has a bug, or the theory's assumptions don't hold in your scenarios.
If you find genuine discrepancies (e.g., theory predicts bias=0 but simulation
shows bias=0.05 in small samples), that's a valuable finding—report it!

REFERENCE: Slide 10 (Worked Example - formula vs simulation)


Q38: How do I know if my simulation results will generalize to real data?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ANSWER: You don't, unless you validate on real data. Simulation results are
conditional on your DGM being realistic. Strategies to increase generalizability:
- Calibrate DGM parameters to literature, pilot data, or expert opinion
- Test sensitivity across a wide range of scenarios (don't just test best-case)
- Include realistic nuisance features (missingness, measurement error,
  heterogeneity)
- Validate on real datasets when possible (after simulation study is complete)

Think of simulation as a "stress test" for methods. If a method performs well
in simulation and on real data, you have converging evidence. If it performs
well in simulation but poorly on real data, your DGM may have missed key
features of reality—that's useful feedback for refining the DGM.

REFERENCE: Slide 6 (DGM - choose realism), Slide 14 (Sensitivity & Robustness)


Q39: Isn't running thousands of simulations a waste of computational resources?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ANSWER: It's an investment in rigor. The computational cost of simulation is
usually trivial compared to the cost of fielding a poorly-designed real study
or deploying a flawed method in practice. Modern computing makes even complex
simulations feasible—what took days 20 years ago now runs in hours on a laptop,
or minutes on a cluster. Plus, simulation studies are highly reusable: once
you've built the infrastructure, you can easily extend it to test new methods
or scenarios. Consider the alternative: publishing a method without adequate
validation, only to have it fail in practice. The cost of that failure (wasted
research dollars, wrong conclusions, harm to patients) far exceeds the cost of
simulation.

REFERENCE: Slide 16 (Practical Concerns - runtime)


Q40: What if I find that all methods perform poorly in some scenarios?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ANSWER: That's a valuable finding! It tells the field, "In this setting,
current methods are inadequate—we need new approaches." Frame it constructively:
- Explain what makes this scenario challenging (small sample + high
  dimensionality? Extreme missingness?)
- Quantify how poorly methods perform (bias, coverage, power)
- Discuss potential solutions (larger samples, different estimands, Bayesian
  priors, alternative study designs)

This kind of honesty advances the field. For example, early simulation studies
showed that standard methods for survival analysis with interval censoring had
poor coverage in small samples—this spurred development of better methods. Your
simulation revealing gaps is the first step toward filling them.

REFERENCE: Slide 15-17 (Use Cases - method comparison, model misspecification)

Q41: What's the minimum number of scenarios I should test?
ANSWER: At least 3-5 scenarios covering low/medium/high values of key factors
(sample size, effect size, nuisance complexity). Avoid testing just one
"representative" scenario—methods often behave differently at extremes.

Q42: Should I report non-significant results from my simulation?
ANSWER: YES. Report all pre-specified comparisons. If two methods don't differ
significantly (accounting for MCSE), say so—that's important information.

Q43: Can I use simulation to justify sample size for my real study?
ANSWER: Yes—that's exactly what the worked example demonstrates. Simulation-
based sample size is especially useful when formulas are unavailable or rely
on unrealistic assumptions.

Q44: How do I handle outliers in simulation results?
ANSWER: Investigate them. Outliers might reveal edge cases where methods fail.
Report outlier rates as a robustness metric. Don't silently remove them unless
they're due to coding errors.

Q45: Can I simulate longitudinal or survival data?
ANSWER: Absolutely. For survival, use parametric distributions (Weibull,
log-logistic) or Cox models. For longitudinal, use random effects or GEEs.
ADEMP applies to all study types.

Q46: What if I don't have time for a full factorial design?
ANSWER: Use a fractional factorial or prioritize scenarios. Pre-specify which
are primary (to answer your main aim) vs exploratory. But don't cut corners on
nsim—too few reps makes MCSEs large and results unreliable.

Q47: Should I blind myself to results while adding scenarios?
ANSWER: Ideally yes, to avoid bias. Or pre-register all scenarios. If you must
add scenarios mid-study, label them post-hoc and justify why they're important.

Q48: How do I cite a simulation study properly?
ANSWER: Cite the original ADEMP paper (Morris et al. 2019) and any guidelines
you followed (e.g., STRESS). Describe your design briefly and point to
supplementary materials or code repository for details.

Q49: Can I use AI/ChatGPT to help write simulation code?
ANSWER: Use with caution. AI can generate boilerplate, but often makes subtle
statistical errors (wrong variance formulas, incorrect test assumptions). Always
validate AI-generated code against known test cases. ADEMP helps here—clear
specification of DGM and estimands makes it easier to spot AI mistakes.

Q50: What's the single most important takeaway from ADEMP?
ANSWER: **Transparency.** Document your aims, data generation, estimands,
methods, and performance measures upfront. Pre-specify when possible. Report
everything, including failures and negative results. Reproducibility and
honesty build trust—simulation studies should be exemplars of rigor, not
vehicles for method salesmanship.
